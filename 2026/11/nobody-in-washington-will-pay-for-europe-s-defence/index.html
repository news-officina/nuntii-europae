Nobody in Washington will pay for Europe's defence

The midterms confirm what was already true: Congress controls little that matters, earmarked money is intent rather than commitment, and the Pentagon is expected to hand Europe's defence to Europe. Member states should budget as if American money were not coming.

7 November 2026

Nobody in Washington will pay for Europe's defence. That is the judgement the midterm results confirm, not overturn. On Tuesday American voters gave Democrats both the House of Representatives, the lower chamber of Congress, and a Senate majority of 54 seats with two races still open, and by 10:00 CET on Wednesday morning European defence stocks had fallen while the wider market rose: BAE Systems down 5.2 percent, Hensoldt down 4.9 percent, Rheinmetall down 3.2 percent. Investors were pricing in slower growth in American military spending. They were right to, and the governments of Europe should read the same signal.

The change in Washington is real, but for European security it changes almost nothing that was not already true. The Pentagon's force posture review for Europe, expected by December 2026, likely shifts conventional military responsibility in NATO towards European ownership to free resources for the Indo-Pacific. The US undersecretary of war for policy, Elbridge Colby, calls this NATO 3.0. A Democratic Congress does not write that review, and Congress has set a statutory floor on American troop numbers in Europe that President Donald Trump contests. Whoever wins an election, the plan is for Europe to run Europe's conventional defence. What has not changed is the budgeting, which still treats American troops and American money as guaranteed.

What a friendlier Congress actually delivers is less than it looks. Euronews reports that the tariff threat will survive this election, because after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs in February 2026 the administration moved them under Section 301, which lets the president act without a vote by lawmakers. Earmarked aid is even more fragile than the tariff threat. ECFR policy fellow Nevada Joan Lee advises Europeans to treat money a Congress writes into law as intent, not commitment, and the record supports her: earlier in 2026 the Pentagon delayed 400 million dollars in Ukraine assistance for months despite congressional authorization. A cheque the executive holds back is not money Europe has.

Defenders of waiting argue that the election has strengthened Washington's commitment: Democrats can attach Ukraine measures to must-pass bills such as the annual defence authorisation, daring President Trump to veto them, which Daniel Hamilton of the Brookings Institution, a research group in Washington, considers unlikely. That is the strongest version of the case, and it mistakes law for action. The troop floor exists on paper while the president contests it, and the 400 million dollars already authorised sat undelivered for months. A floor a sitting president does not accept is not a guarantee. Rushed spending risks waste, but the waste to fear is buying what an ally may withdraw.

The election settled who controls Congress, not who pays for Europe's safety. What authorisation is worth, the withheld Ukraine money has already shown. Europe's capitals should write their budgets for conventional defence as if American money were not coming, and the Council, where EU member state governments meet, should agree those budgets before the review is published in December 2026. Whoever wins in Washington, the chequebook for European security sits in the capitals of Europe.
