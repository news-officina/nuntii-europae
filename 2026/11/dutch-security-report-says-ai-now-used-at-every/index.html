Dutch security report says AI now used at every stage of cyber attacks

The NCTV warns it is a matter of when, not whether, the Netherlands faces a society-disrupting incident, and names China, Russia, Iran and North Korea as threats.

7 November 2026, updated 8 November 2026

Cyber actors demonstrably use artificial intelligence at every stage of their operations, according to the NCTV, the Dutch national coordinator for security and counterterrorism, in its annual report Cybersecuritybeeld Nederland 2026, published on 6 November. The report shows this for the first time. It states that AI makes cyberattacks faster, and adds to their scale and efficiency.

According to the NCTV, it is not a question of whether but when the Netherlands will face a society-disrupting cyber incident, partly as a result of technological developments. The centre says China, Russia, Iran and North Korea have set their sights on the country. Russian groups, it states, attack European and Dutch targets continuously, for example to gather intelligence on the war in Ukraine and to create unrest among NATO allies.

In most cases, the centre found, AI still plays a supporting role: it helps attackers find unknown vulnerabilities faster, automate exploitation and develop malware. Increasingly, it also acts during attacks themselves, with malware communicating with an AI model while an attack is under way. The NCTV concluded that the capabilities and activities of state actors and cybercriminals are rising, while the digital resilience of Dutch organisations is not growing at the same pace. The head of the centre compared it to a Grand Canyon that must be crossed.

The NCTV report documents concrete attacks as well. According to the NCTV, Russian hackers attempted to gain access to the Signal and WhatsApp accounts of Dutch civil servants. Russian state actors also targeted internet-accessible IP cameras in the Netherlands, the centre states, to collect militarily relevant data, such as military transport routes and the logistics of matériel deliveries to Ukraine. Iran, the report says, deployed advanced malware in espionage campaigns against dissidents and journalists critical of the regime in Tehran, including critics living in the West.

Kuipers calls news very bad

Marc Kuipers, the NCTV coordinator, called the news in the report very bad. He compared the Netherlands to frogs in a pan of water whose temperature rises by a degree each day. The country, he said, should not wait for a conflict with Russia to start working on its resilience now. He added that the overall picture points to a state actor waging an aggressive campaign against the West, though he cannot attribute specific incidents to Russia.

The Chinese embassy in the Netherlands rejected the Dutch cybersecurity advisory when it was published in early October. It said the document seriously damaged China's image and contained factually inaccurate allegations without foundation. The embassy added that China is itself among the main victims of cyber attacks and acts against malicious cyber activities in accordance with the law. It also called for international cooperation based on mutual respect and equality, and urged the Dutch authorities to stop making groundless accusations under the pretext of cybersecurity.

The Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure (DIVD) attributed the September 2026 attack on its network to the autonomous ARTEX AI. The system, the DIVD said, chained a Zammad zero-day with a second zero-day for local privilege escalation. It moved from initial access to root privileges in 12 minutes. It recorded more than 450,000 requests and 2,500 scripts during the attack. The institute described the activity as "super loud and messy."

Earlier advice from services

The warnings from the report follow earlier advice from the Dutch services and the Cyber Security Raad. In early October, the AIVD, the Dutch general intelligence service, the MIVD, Dutch military intelligence, and the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) issued a joint advisory. The services warned that Chinese cyber attacks exploiting vulnerable edge devices were likely to increase substantially in the coming years. In March, the Cyber Security Raad, the Dutch government's cybersecurity advisory council, had already told the cabinet that AI-driven cyber attacks form a full strategic threat.

On 6 November the Dutch cabinet, coordinated by the NCTV, presented an action programme to strengthen digital resilience. It includes an 'AI-wasstraat', a screening service that checks systems for digital vulnerabilities, and measures to prepare small and medium-sized businesses better for incidents. Separately, the AIVD confirmed that the Cyberbeveiligingswet, a Dutch cybersecurity law now in force, obliges organisations under it to control their cyber risks. Responsibility for that, the service stated, lies explicitly with administrators.
