Male State founder denies involvement in European flight bomb threats

Vladislav Pozdniakov said on Telegram that the far-right group no longer exists, as alerts again disrupted flights on 6 November. The Rome Prosecutor's Office has opened an investigation.

6 November 2026

Vladislav Pozdniakov, founder of the Russian far-right movement Male State, denied on 6 November that the group was involved in the bomb threats that disrupted European flights and media offices. Writing on Telegram, he said the movement no longer exists. The denial came as a fresh wave of alerts hit air traffic that day; affected aircraft landed safely and searches found no explosive devices.

The alerts in the week before 6 November hit dozens of European flights, including Aer Lingus and Ryanair services, as well as Irish media offices including RTÉ, Ireland's public broadcaster, and Newstalk, an Irish radio station, and some supermarkets. The bomb threats of Tuesday and Thursday forced at least 35 flights to be diverted or searched. On 3 November, several flights made emergency landings after explosives hoaxes. The Irish government of Micheál Martin, the Taoiseach, as Ireland's prime minister is called, judged it probable that the false alerts were a coordinated campaign aimed at causing disruption.

The threats targeting Rome had arrived by email. One message, sent at 07:21 on 5 November to Aeroporti di Roma and Fiumicino airport, claimed Male State fighters had planted explosives on two incoming flights, a Finnair service from Helsinki and an Aer Lingus flight from Dublin. The email said this was an attack on Italy's feminist regime. The email was written in word-perfect Italian, and its originating address appeared linked to Bogota and Colombia's state health services. The Aer Lingus plane was diverted to Évreux military air base in France.

A separate email, sent to several newsrooms on 6 November, was signed by a self-styled Mikhail Andropov, who presented himself as one of the leaders of the group behind the recent bomb threats. Its authenticity and origin were not established. The message warned in its own words that a new wave of attacks was being prepared, and that aeroplanes, cruise ships, metro stations and trains would soon be blown up. Spain's foreign minister, José Manuel Albares, told reporters that the most reasonable assumption was that Europe faced an attempt at hybrid aggression, and that the continent should expect more such challenges.

The Rome Prosecutor's Office has opened an investigation into the threats against Italian flights and airports, without ruling out terrorism charges. In earlier incidents, Hungary sent Gripen fighter jets and Bulgaria sent MiGs to escort planes that had been targeted. Investigators have so far found no evidence that a hostile state or terror group, such as Russia or Iran, was behind the hoaxes, though inquiries continue.
