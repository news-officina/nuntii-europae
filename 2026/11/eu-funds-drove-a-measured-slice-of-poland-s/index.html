EU funds drove a measured slice of Poland's growth, and the bill is now due

Poland's economy doubled since EU accession, but the government's own evaluation attributes only a modest share of that rise to EU money, while the deficit climbs. Both the gains and the costs now carry numbers as the next EU budget is negotiated.

2 November 2026

Poland grew faster between 2004 and 2021 than almost any other country in the European Union. Statistics Poland, the national statistics office, put cumulative real GDP growth at 99.1 percent over those years, against an EU average of 25.2 percent, the third highest of the 27 member states. The OECD, in its own assessment, found the economy had doubled in size over two decades, growing faster than neighbouring Central European countries and at twice its own average.

Statistics Poland marked those two decades with a publication of its own, and conceded the limits of the exercise within it. "Depicting the full spectrum of changes in a concise form is an almost impossible task," the office wrote.

The concession reaches further than description. What the publication does not state, and what no single figure states, is how much of that growth was produced by EU money.

Freedom House, the US-based democracy monitor, found that defenders of illiberalism in the EU enjoy hundreds of millions of dollars in cohesion funds with minimal accountability. The same organisation reported that every country in Central Europe has seen its democracy score decline since 2008.

Money explains part of the record. It does not explain all of it.

The ministry's number

The Ministry of Development Funds and Regional Policy has put a number on it. Its evaluation compares the years 2004 to 2021 as they happened with a counterfactual scenario built without EU funds, drawing on data from Statistics Poland, the ministry's own studies and Eurostat.

On that measure, projects co-financed from EU funds accounted for about 8.7 percent of Poland's average annual GDP growth over the period. The money behind it was large. Cohesion Policy funds used in Poland by the end of 2021 amounted to about 621.9 billion zloty, and annual payments averaged 2.0 percent of nominal GDP.

Romania, Malta and Bulgaria are missing from the cross-country comparison, because data for those countries was unavailable, the portal notes.

The period average of 3.9 percent annual growth, against 1.3 percent in the EU-27, stemmed to a significant extent, but not wholly, from the funds, the portal notes. In 2015 to 2018 the impact turned technically negative, as the 2007-2013 budget cycle ended before the next one was running at scale. Only 13 to 15 percent of the narrowed distance to the EU average came from Cohesion Policy investment in the richest regions.

The fiscal price

The growth has had a fiscal price, according to the International Monetary Fund's report of 9 January 2026. It projects Poland's deficit at 7 percent of GDP in 2025. Convergence, the report finds, came at the cost of a deteriorating fiscal position.

The IMF notes that two rating agencies have turned their outlook on Poland to negative. Markets have barely moved, the IMF notes, apart from a marginal widening in asset swap spreads.

The costs are now meeting the budget. In the negotiating box for the next long-term budget, member states bordering Russia or Belarus receive an increased weighting in the border-management allocation, a line that bears directly on Poland.

Poland has its own objections. Its authorities state the planned ETS2 carbon pricing unfairly shifts the burden of decarbonisation to lower income countries and should not proceed in its current design, the IMF records.

The OECD noted that the Civic Coalition, the Third Way and the Left have governed Poland together since December 2023, with priorities including unblocking and expediting EU Recovery and Resilience funding.

The loss claim

In September 2021, MEP Patryk Jaki presented a report in the Sejm, Poland's parliament, that reached the opposite conclusion. It weighed what Poland paid in against what it received over 16 years of membership and put the total at a loss of 535 billion zloty.

The report's methods drew criticism. Maciej Bukowski, head of the think tank Wise Europa, said the authors either did not understand the operations they performed on the numbers or wanted to manipulate readers.

The report listed Zbigniew Krysiak of SGH, the Warsaw School of Economics, as a co-author. The university publicly distanced itself from the work. As a co-author, Krysiak did not represent SGH, and the report was produced outside the university and his employment duties, it said in a statement. For that reason, it said, the university could not comment on the report itself.

Neither claim holds

The two claims on the table both fail the record. Poland's growth did not come mainly from EU money: the ministry's evaluation attributes most of the convergence in the richest regions to other sources, and warns against crediting accession with the whole result. But neither did membership bankrupt the country.

The sums behind the case for membership and the case against it are measured, and they sit in the same frame. A modest, audited share of growth is attributable to the funds, NIK's past audits confirmed effective spending, and a fiscal cost now stands beside the gains. Agriculture shows the same pattern, an SGH study finds, with rising payments needed to maintain income parity that has not been reached.

What remains is a question with numbers on both sides, and a budget negotiation that will weigh them.

What happens to the 1.25 border factor next sits in the negotiating box for the next long-term budget, a text the Council has not agreed.

IMF staff assess that neither EU nor domestic fiscal rules are likely to require additional adjustment in Poland before 2029 due to activation of the defence escape clause.
