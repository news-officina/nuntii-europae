Ukrainian firm builds 290-gram receiver that turns Russian drone signals into live video

The device from Kyiv company CODE380 tracks the frequency band used by Russia's Molniya drones since June 2026. Specialists caution its advantage may fade if Russia shifts fully to digital video.

3 November 2026

The Kyiv-based communications company CODE380 has built a pocket-sized software-defined radio receiver that turns signals from Russian drones into video a soldier can watch in real time. The device, called the D·Vision SDR, detects drone signals and converts them into a viewable feed. Its sweep covers the 4.1 to 4.5 GHz band, which Russia's Molniya strike drones began using for video transmission in June 2026.

Unlike conventional detectors that rely on analog receivers, the D·Vision SDR analyzes the radio airwaves through software. That design lets CODE380 issue software updates when Russian forces switch drone frequencies, so the receiver can follow them.

At about 290 grams, the D·Vision SDR weighs roughly half as much as competing Ukrainian devices, according to three outlets that have reviewed it, including Defense News and Militarnyi. It can detect PAL and NTSC analog video signals, as well as digital signals from DJI Mavic drones, at distances of up to 2.5 km, Militarnyi reported. Babai's Vydun 7.5 weighs 0.9 kg and reaches 7.5 GHz, Contra-drone's SENSE-4 600 g and 8.2 GHz, BlueBird Tech's Chuyka 3.0 up to 650 g and 8.8 GHz.

The D·Vision SDR was tested in field conditions, including with Ukrainian service members during combat missions, according to Dou.ua, Militarnyi and TechRadar. It has also faced a moving target. Beskrestnov, a Ukrainian electronic-warfare specialist, said Russia is designing drone variants with harder-to-hack video feeds, pointing to the Molniya. Whether software updates alone can keep pace with such feeds remains an open question.

Experts warned that the edge may not last. Beskrestnov and other specialists said that if Russia fully switches to digital video streams and fiber-optic control, portable analog detectors and fixed receiving stations would become ineffective. "We need to be ready for digital video to one day fully replace analog," he said, and Ukrainian makers are now building upgrade capability into new devices. The trade publication Whalesbook judged that the technology gives a temporary tactical advantage, with its long-term utility at risk from evolving digital encryption.
